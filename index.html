Why free BPM tools still matter

Tempo is the shared language of DJs, dancers, and producers. When you only need a quick read of an MP3 or WAV, installing a full DAW is slow. Browser-based BPM detection analyzes audio locally with Web Audio APIs so unreleased demos do not have to leave your device.

The full guide explains confidence scores, half-time and double-time, tap tempo, and how to use a free BPM result in a setlist or practice loop.

Independent streaming for original music

Major distributors are useful, but they are not the only path. Independent artists can host free listen experiences, publish an ISRC-aware catalog, and still list on Spotify or YouTube Music. You control masters, alternate free streams, and how you talk to listeners.

Many day-to-day tasks only need a focused free tool: tempo check, a public listen page, resonance experiments. We document tools we actually maintain so the advice matches the product.

What this site is

DeepSeek Oracle is the guide side of the workshop. It publishes practical writing about free tools, independent music, and the parts of a creator workflow that do not need a subscription. The tools themselves live on their own domains; this site explains how to use them and when not to.

The site is deliberately small. There are three long guides, an about page, and a systems hub for power users. The point is not to cover every topic; it is to answer the questions that come up when someone is actually trying to finish a track or publish a page.

The guides

The BPM detection guide covers tempo detection in the browser, including when the number is wrong. The independent streaming guide explains the difference between a platform feed and a direct listening room. The free tools guide is the map of utilities that do not require an account.

How to use it

Start with the question you already have. If you are checking a tempo, open the BPM guide and then the tool. If you are deciding where to publish, open the streaming guide. If you are comparing tools, open the free tools guide and follow the links to the canonical pages. The systems hub is for the deeper LYGO material, not for a first read.

Support

Advertising on the guide pages helps cover hosting when the site is approved. Optional support through PayPal.me/ExcavationPro is never required to read a guide or use a free tool.

A small publisher, not a content farm

The site publishes a few long pages instead of a feed of short posts. A guide is added when it answers a question that the tools cannot answer by themselves, and it is updated when the tool or the rule changes. The small size is the quality control: every page has a job, and a page that loses its job is revised rather than padded.

If you want the practical route, open a guide and follow the tool link. If you want the deeper systems material, use the hub. If you only want to know who is behind it, the about page is short on purpose.
